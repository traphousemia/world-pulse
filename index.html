Frequently Asked Questions

How is the live population number calculated?

There is no sensor network counting every birth and death on Earth in real time — no organization has that. Instead, this counter starts from the most recent official population estimate published by the World Bank (sourced from the UN Population Division) and advances it forward using the average annual birth and death rates for that same year, converted into a per-second rate. It's the same method used by long-running population clocks like the U.S. Census Bureau's World Population Clock: a statistically grounded projection, not a live census.

Why do different population counters show slightly different numbers?

Every population clock relies on a baseline estimate and a growth rate, and different organizations (the UN, the U.S. Census Bureau, Worldometer, and others) update their baselines on different schedules and sometimes use slightly different methodologies for estimating birth and death rates in countries with incomplete civil registration data. The differences are typically a fraction of a percent — meaningful at the level of exact digits, but not at the level of the overall trend.

Is global population growth speeding up or slowing down?

Slowing down, and has been for decades. The global growth rate peaked at roughly 2.3% per year in 1963 and has fallen to under 0.9% today. Birth rates have declined faster than death rates in most regions as education, healthcare access, and urbanization have increased — a pattern demographers call the "demographic transition." The UN's median projection has global population peaking around the mid-2080s before beginning a gradual decline.

What's the difference between the birth rate and the fertility rate?

The crude birth rate measures live births per 1,000 people in the total population per year — it's simple but sensitive to a country's age structure. The total fertility rate measures the average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime given current age-specific birth rates. A population needs a fertility rate of roughly 2.1 births per woman to replace itself without immigration; the current global average sits just above that line, though many individual countries are now well below it.

Where does the data on this page come from?

Population totals and demographic rates are sourced from the World Bank's Open Data platform (indicators SP.POP.TOTL, SP.DYN.CBRT.IN, and SP.DYN.CDRT.IN), which in turn draws on the UN Population Division's World Population Prospects — the standard reference dataset used by researchers, governments, and international agencies worldwide. Full source links are listed in the footer below.

What is the world death toll today?

Based on the current UN/World Bank annual death rate, an estimated 170,000+ people die worldwide each day — roughly 2 people per second. You can see this ticking in real time in the "Deaths Today" figure above, and broken down by leading cause (cardiovascular disease, cancer, accidents, suicide, and more) in the World Death Toll table on this page.

What causes the most deaths worldwide?

Cardiovascular disease (heart disease and stroke) is the leading cause of death globally, accounting for roughly a third of all deaths, according to WHO Global Health Estimates. Cancer is second, followed by chronic respiratory diseases. Diabetes, road traffic accidents, suicide, and homicide are individually smaller but still substantial contributors — see the full breakdown above.

How many people die from war, suicide, or violence each year?

These figures vary far more year to year than disease-related causes. The World Health Organization estimates roughly 700,000+ suicides and 400,000+ homicide/violence deaths globally per year. War and armed conflict deaths are the most volatile of all — the Uppsala Conflict Data Program has recorded anywhere from under 50,000 to well over 200,000 battle-related deaths in a single year depending on how many major conflicts are active, which is why we show it as a range rather than a steady live count.

Does World Pulse have a population heat map?

Yes. The spinning 3D globe on this page is a live population-density heat map, shading every country by how densely populated it is, from sparse (blue) to extremely dense (red). Click "High Detail" for a full interactive globe with real day/night satellite-style imagery, per-country growth rates, and a searchable list of every country's live population, birth rate, and death rate.

Can I see the population of a specific country in real time?

Yes — use the country dropdown near the globe (or the search box in High Detail mode) to switch the live counters to any of 218 countries and territories. Each one shows its own live population estimate, today's births and deaths, and net growth per second, computed the same way as the world total: World Bank/UN baseline data projected forward using that country's birth and death rates.
