DRC Ebola Outbreak Becomes Fastest-Spreading on Record

Published August 4, 2026 · By World Pulse · Reporting built on data from CDC and WHO situation reports

An outbreak of Bundibugyo ebolavirus in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo has become the fastest-spreading Ebola epidemic ever recorded, according to CDC and WHO situation reports. As of August 4, 2026, the outbreak has been linked to 3,895 confirmed cases and 1,753 deaths — a case fatality rate of roughly 45%. This World Pulse report explains why the strain matters for the public-health response and how the outbreak reads against the broader global mortality picture.

Where the outbreak is concentrated

The epidemic remains centered in Ituri Province, which accounts for the large majority of confirmed cases, with smaller clusters in North Kivu, Haut-Uélé, Tshopo, and South Kivu provinces. Limited cross-border transmission has also been reported in Uganda and a single recovered case in France, though the outbreak has not spread widely beyond the DRC's eastern provinces.

Why this strain has complicated the response

The outbreak is caused by Bundibugyo ebolavirus (BDBV), a less common Ebola species than the Zaire ebolavirus responsible for most prior major outbreaks, including the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic. Existing Ebola vaccines and treatments were developed and certified specifically against the Zaire strain, which has complicated containment efforts and contributed to the outbreak's unusually fast spread relative to past epidemics.

A related outbreak has ended

In more encouraging news, Uganda's Ministry of Health declared its own linked Ebola outbreak over on July 28, 2026, after 42 days passed without a new confirmed locally transmitted case — the standard threshold health authorities use to confirm an outbreak's end.

What this means for the global death toll

At current figures, this single outbreak accounts for a meaningful, if geographically contained, share of this year's infectious-disease deaths — a reminder that the baseline global death toll World Pulse tracks (built from steady annual rates for causes like cardiovascular disease and cancer) doesn't capture the deaths from active outbreaks like this one, which is exactly why acute events like this are tracked separately rather than folded into a steady per-second rate.

Why World Pulse is tracking this outbreak

World Pulse is built to make population and mortality trends legible in real time, and an outbreak like this one exposes the limits of that approach in a useful way. A per-second global death rate is smooth and steady precisely because it averages out the spikes that dominate headlines — yet those spikes are where the human story is. Tracking the DRC Ebola outbreak separately lets us show both layers at once: the calm baseline and the active emergency. We will continue to update this page as CDC and WHO publish revised case and death counts, and we report the strain-specific vaccine gap because it is the single factor most likely to determine how — and how quickly — this epidemic ends.
